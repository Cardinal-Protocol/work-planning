What we're building

A yield perpetual ("yield perp") is a trading product where, instead of placing a bet and closing it out once for a single win or loss, a trader makes a standing bet about how a price will behave — and gets paid continuously, like interest, for every moment that bet holds up. They keep getting paid (or keep paying) tick by tick until they choose to close, or until they run out of margin.

There's no other trader on the other side of that bet. A shared pool of capital from liquidity providers ("LPs") is the permanent counterparty for every position, every trader, all the time. When traders lose, the pool earns; when traders win, the pool pays out of that same shared capital.

For this first version, trading happens with faucet-minted play money as part of a time-boxed competition — whoever grows their balance the most by the deadline wins a real prize, paid out manually by the team afterward. That's what lets us skip a lot of the machinery a real-money version would need (real hedging, multiple oracles, a multisig) while keeping the actual trading mechanics real.

Three ways to bet ("seats")

Range

"Price stays roughly where it is." The trader picks a band around today's price and earns for every moment price stays inside it — loses, faster and faster, the further price wanders outside.

Long

"Price goes up, or at least doesn't fall too far too fast." A line starts just below today's price and slowly climbs toward it over time. The trader earns while price stays above that line — loses once price falls to or below it.

Short

The mirror of Long: the line starts just above today's price and falls toward it. The trader earns while price stays below the line.

Margin, leverage, and getting paid

A trader puts up real capital ("margin") and picks a leverage multiplier — for this launch, 25× or 100×. Leverage scales up the size of the bet (the "notional") without requiring more capital, which means bigger swings in both directions, and a proportionally tighter band or line to match — higher leverage is a sharper, faster-moving bet, not just a bigger one.

Every few seconds, a backend engine checks whether the price is currently inside or outside the trader's condition and credits or debits the position a small amount — scaled by how deep inside/outside price is, and by how volatile the market currently is compared to when the position opened. Calmer-than-usual markets slow everything down (both earning and draining); more volatile-than-usual markets speed everything up.

Periodically — every time a position's net gain or loss crosses a fixed threshold, called an epoch — two things happen: the gain earned so far is paid into the trader's withdrawable balance, and a small funding fee is charged for that epoch. Older versions of this design charged that fee as one lump sum at the epoch boundary, which could feel jarring (a trader could be doing fine and then watch a chunk disappear all at once). This build uses carry-forward settlement instead: each boundary charges one small, fixed-size slice and remembers whatever's left over for the next one — smoother, and the total charged is identical either way.

The user's actual journey

Every step is either something the user does with their wallet, something the backend computes and signs, or something that actually happens on-chain. Most of a position's life (step 4) touches none of the three — it's just numbers changing in a database.

#WhoWhat happens
1UserConnects a wallet, picks a seat (Range/Long/Short), a leverage tier, and how much to commit. The frontend shows a live fee quote before they commit to anything.
2UserSigns an intent with their wallet — a structured, human-readable message ("Open a 100× Long, $500 committed, max 40bps entry fee") saying what they want to do. No gas paid yet; this is just a signature.
3BackendComputes the exact fee and opening terms at this instant, co-signs its own confirmation of those terms (a Quote), and submits one blockchain transaction that checks both signatures and executes the open.
4ChainPulls the trader's margin into the shared vault and records the bare minimum on-chain: who opened it, how much margin is locked, and that it's now "open." No gas cost to the trader beyond this one transaction.
5Backend(Ongoing, every few seconds) Checks the position against the live price and credits or debits it inside the database. Free, instant, no blockchain involved — this is what lets the product tick continuously without a transaction fee every few seconds.
6Backend(Periodically, at each epoch) Pays the accrued gain into the trader's withdrawable balance and charges that epoch's fee from margin — or parks the position (flows pause, nothing closes) if margin can't cover it.
7User → Backend → ChainWhenever the trader wants out, they sign a close intent; the backend prices the final PnL, countersigns, and submits the closing transaction. Margin plus any gains are released.
8User → ChainSeparately, at any time, the trader can pull their already-earned withdrawable balance out of the vault. In this accelerated plan, that step is admin-processed rather than fully self-serve — see the withdrawal explainer in the Backend track below.

Track A — Contracts

owner Steve · 31 hrs itemized (was 90) @ 10 hr/wk

Everything expensive to compute — pricing, per-tick accrual, funding — happens off-chain, in the backend. The blockchain's only job is to be the trust anchor for the few moments actual money needs to move: locking a trader's margin when they open, paying it back out when they close, and releasing earned funds on withdrawal. That narrow job is split across exactly two contracts.

Vault + ERC-4626

YieldPerpVault is the one place that ever actually holds money — every trader's margin and all of the LPs' pooled capital. LPs deposit the test collateral token and receive a receipt token back representing their share of the pool; that receipt's value rises and falls with the pool's overall performance.

This exact pattern — pool deposits, issue proportional shares, redeem shares for a proportional cut of the pool — is common enough on Ethereum that there's a standard interface for it: ERC-4626, the "tokenized vault" standard. Using it means every wallet, block explorer, and integrator already knows how to read this contract's balances and shares without custom code. Solady (a gas-optimized contract library already vendored in this repo) ships a ready-built ERC-4626 implementation, so this task is wiring it up with the right parameters, not writing share math from scratch.

Settlement contract + the "satellite" pattern

YieldPerpSettlement holds none of the money — it holds the decision logic: whose signatures are valid, what a position's current status is (open / parked / closed), and how much margin is locked against it. When it needs to actually move funds, it calls back into the Vault through a narrow, restricted interface rather than touching tokens itself.

The reason for splitting these two apart: the contract that custodies everyone's money should be as simple and unchanging as possible, since a bug there is catastrophic. The contract with more complex, more likely-to-evolve logic (which is exactly what Settlement is) can be swapped out or upgraded later without the Vault — or anyone's actual funds — ever being touched.

Signed intents + EIP-712

Every action a trader takes needs their explicit, unambiguous authorization. The naive way to do that — sign a raw blob of bytes with your wallet — is bad UX (you can't read what you're agreeing to) and a phishing risk (you can't easily tell a legitimate signature request from a malicious one). EIP-712 is the Ethereum standard that fixes this: it lets a wallet show the user a readable, labeled message — trader, action, leverage, amount, expiry — instead of gibberish, and produces a signature the contract can later verify came from exactly that person, over exactly those terms.

Because pricing changes every few seconds, a trader's signature alone ("I want to open this") isn't enough proof of what price it should execute at. So the backend's own key (the "operator") co-signs a second message — a Quote — locking in the exact terms at execution time. The contract checks both signatures before moving anything, so neither side can quietly change the deal after the fact.

The governance fee table

Every leverage tier has its own row in a small table of numbers that make up that tier's fee — an amount taken when a position opens and again at every funding epoch. Storing this table on-chain with an admin-only update function means the team can retune fees as real competition data comes in (win rates, market conditions) without redeploying any contract. Only 2 rows ship at launch (25× and 100×) — see the cuts table below.

Admission cap

Before letting any new position open, the contract checks that the pool's total worst-case exposure across every open position stays under a fixed slice of the pool's total value (its NAV — net asset value: LP capital, adjusted for what traders currently owe or are owed). This keeps the pool from ever taking on more risk than its capital could plausibly cover.

Track B — Backend

owner Leifu · 107 hrs (was 150) @ ~35 hr/wk

This is where nearly all of the actual product logic lives. Checking every open position's price condition every few seconds is far too slow and expensive to do on a blockchain directly — so instead, a normal database (Postgres) holds the live truth about every position, and a set of always-running services keep it correct and act on it. The blockchain only hears about the moments money actually moves.

The pricing/accrual "tick engine"

This is the heart of the product — a service that wakes up every few seconds and, for every open position, checks the live price against that position's band or line and adds or subtracts a small amount from its running total. It's also what implements the position-time "clock" for Long/Short seats (the line's climb speeds up in a more volatile market than the one the position opened in, slows down in a calmer one) and the carry-forward funding math described above. Because this is the one piece of code that literally determines who owes what, it's the least-cut item in this whole plan.

Realized-volatility feed

The engine above needs one live number: how volatile the index currently is, compared to when a position was opened. That's computed as a rolling average of recent price swings that weights recent moves more heavily than older ones (an EWMA — exponentially-weighted moving average), so it reacts to real shifts in market conditions without jumping around on every single tick.

Intent executor

The service that watches for signed intents arriving from the frontend, checks them against the live tick engine's numbers, computes the exact fee, signs the operator's half of the Quote (see the EIP-712 explainer in Track A), and submits the one blockchain transaction that actually executes the action. This is the bridge between "a user clicked a button and signed a message" and "money moved on-chain."

Withdrawals — how a self-serve claim would work, and why we're cutting it here

A trader's earned balance lives only in the database until they withdraw it. The trust-minimized way to let someone pull that money out — even if the backend or team ever went dark — uses a structure called a Merkle tree: take every trader's balance, hash them together in pairs, over and over, until it collapses into one 32-byte fingerprint (the "root"). Post just that single root on-chain — cheap, one number, no matter how many traders there are. To actually withdraw, a trader submits their own balance plus a short "proof" (a handful of the intermediate hashes from the tree) that lets the contract recompute the root on the spot and check it matches what was posted. If it matches, that balance is provably part of the committed set, and the contract pays out — with no backend involved at claim time.

Building and serving those proofs is genuinely useful, but it's also real engineering effort for a property (withdraw even if the team disappears) that matters much less over a short, team-run competition with a manually-paid prize. So for this accelerated plan, withdrawals are admin-triggered instead: the team processes them directly against the database's numbers, no tree or proof required. This is the single highest-priority item to add back before any longer-lived or higher-stakes version of this product — it's what removes the team as a dependency for someone getting their own money out.

Faucet & leaderboard

The faucet is a rate-limited endpoint that mints free test collateral to anyone who asks — since this is a competition, "trading" happens with play money, not real funds. The leaderboard is a simple read-only ranking of every participant's current equity (locked margin plus already-earned balance), read straight from the database — it doesn't need any on-chain source of truth, since the real prize is paid out manually by the team after inspecting the numbers.
